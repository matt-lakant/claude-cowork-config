# Expert Interview Guide

## What this produces
A 30 to 60 minute interview guide with timed sections, questions mapped to hypotheses, follow-up probes, and a one-paragraph opener.

## Inputs to ask for
- The hypotheses to test (from the workplan)
- Interviewee type: frontline, manager, executive, customer, supplier, former employee, outside expert
- Interview length
- Anything the interviewer must not discuss (confidential initiatives, pending changes)
If hypotheses are missing, ask for the key question and draft three to five hypotheses first.

## Method
1. Select the three to five hypotheses this interviewee is best placed to confirm or refute. Drop the rest.
2. Open with context and a confidentiality statement; ask about their role and a typical week.
3. Ask for specific recent instances before opinions: "Walk me through the last time a quote took more than a week." Episodes beat generalizations.
4. Ask for numbers wherever possible: how often, how long, how many, what share.
5. For each hypothesis, include one disconfirming question that gives the interviewee room to say the hypothesis is wrong.
6. Remove leading and double-barreled questions. One question, one idea.
7. For outside experts, add a compliance line: they must not share confidential information from a current or former employer.
8. Close with: "What should I have asked that I didn't?" and "Who else should I talk to?"

## Output format
- Opener (under 80 words)
- Table: Time | Question | Hypothesis tested | Probes | Listen for
- Closing questions
- Note-taking template: Hypothesis | Evidence for | Evidence against | Quote | Number heard

## Quality checks
- Every question maps to a hypothesis or to rapport
- At least one disconfirming question per hypothesis
- No leading or double-barreled questions remain
- Timing adds up to the stated length with 5 minutes spare

## Notes from the source article
- **Replaces:** The interview guides a project team writes before talking to frontline staff, managers, customers, and outside industry experts, each one built to test specific hypotheses instead of collecting opinions.
- **Example request:** “I’m interviewing six dispatchers next week to test whether our scheduling tool is causing overtime. Write me a 45-minute guide.”
- **What the user still owns:** The room. A guide cannot make a nervous dispatcher trust you; that comes from how you show up and whether you protect what they tell you.
- When handing the deliverable back, name in one line the decision above that stays with the user.
